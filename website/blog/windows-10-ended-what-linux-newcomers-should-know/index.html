Windows 10 Support Ended. A Task-Manager Guide for Linux Newcomers

2026-10-11 · sabaoongfx · 3 min read

Microsoft ended support for Windows 10 on October 14, 2025. Machines that can’t run Windows 11 stopped getting free security updates, and that pushed a lot of people to ask whether Linux could be the answer. If you are one of them, the first week is mostly about finding the familiar things in unfamiliar places. A task manager is a good place to start, because it answers the question you will ask most often: “why is my computer slow right now?”

Is everyone really switching?

Not in the numbers, and it is worth being skeptical of big claims. Steam’s October 2025 survey showed Linux crossing 3 percent of Steam users for the first time (3.05 percent), and the November survey put it at 3.2 percent, ahead of macOS on that platform. StatCounter’s all-desktop figures for the United States showed Linux at 3.49 percent in October 2025 after a brief spike above 5 percent in June.

Two caveats apply. Steam’s sample is gamers, who skew technical, and a large part of Linux’s Steam share is Steam Deck hardware. And reporting suggests that most people leaving Windows 10 moved to Windows 11, not to Linux. So Linux is growing, but slowly, and mostly among people who chose it on purpose. If you are trying it, you are still in a small group.

Where things live: Windows to Linux

Resource MonitorThe Performance tab, or a terminal tool such as htop or otm

Your first-week checklist

  1. Install a monitor you recognise. Open Task Manager keeps the Windows 11 layout, so the Processes, Performance, Startup apps and Users tabs are where you expect them. On Ubuntu, Debian, Mint and Pop!_OS it installs from a signed apt repository.
  2. Look at the Processes tab with nothing open. This is your baseline. Note total memory used at idle so you can tell later what changed.
  3. Open your usual apps and watch. Your browser will probably be the biggest item, as it was on Windows.
  4. Check Startup apps. A fresh install may launch things you don’t need at login.
  5. Learn one terminal tool. You will meet SSH and servers eventually, and a text-mode monitor works over both. We compare the options.

Things that will look odd

  • Memory shows as used even when little is running. Linux uses spare RAM as disk cache and gives it back when needed, so “used” is not the same as “in trouble.”
  • You will see processes you don’t recognise. Most are small desktop helpers. Search the name before ending anything.
  • Some actions need administrator rights. Ending another user’s process, or a system one, will be refused unless you elevate.

The point isn’t that Linux is better or worse than Windows. It is that the basic skill of reading what your machine is doing carries over, and a familiar-looking tool makes the move less jarring.
